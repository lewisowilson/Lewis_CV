Q3b, confirmatory

Weather and who wins

Does rain or wind level the field between favourite and underdog?

No evidence

What fans assume

Rain is the great leveller. A heavy pitch and a greasy ball are meant to drag the favourite down to the underdog's level.

The naive view

The favourite is whichever side the strength model rated more likely to win. In dry test matches favourites took 1.88 points per match; with heavier rain in the kick-off hour, 1.66, from only 97 matches.

Controlling for team strength

Against the model's expectation, the pre-registered test found −0.077 expected points per match moving away from the favourite per standard deviation of rain (−0.143 to −0.016). That interval excludes zero before any correction.

The market test

The closing market is a tougher control than our model: it has team news and the money of people paid to be right. With the market's probabilities in place of ours the estimate is −0.074 points per match (−0.138 to −0.012). The interval just excludes zero, but this check never decides the verdict.

The verdict

No evidence

No evidence after correction. Heavier rain moved -0.08 expected points per match away from the favourite per standard deviation (unadjusted p = 0.05, 0.32 after correction).

Unadjusted p = 0.047; after the Holm correction for testing nine things at once, 0.324. Rule fixed in advance in the pre-registration.

What it means

This is the result the pre-registration exists for. On its own it looks like an effect; tested alongside eight other questions it does not survive, and the rule says so. It goes on the list to retest on future seasons, not on the list of findings.

Verdict rule: "Real effect" needs a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05 and a practically meaningful size; "Small effect" is detectable but small; "No evidence" means the adjusted p is 0.05 or more. Methodology.
