Q1, confirmatory

Form

Does recent form predict results once you know how good a team is?

No evidence

What fans assume

Form is everything. A side on a winning run is full of confidence; a side that has lost four on the bounce is in a crisis.

The naive view

Split the 1,520 test matches (2022/23 to 2025/26) into five equal groups by form gap. Home sides in the top group took 1.95 points per match; in the bottom group, 1.09. A gap of +0.86 points, before anything is controlled for.

Controlling for team strength

Now subtract what the strength model (Elo blended with Dixon-Coles) already expected before kick-off. The gap between the top and bottom groups becomes −0.07 points per match.

The pre-registered test puts a number on it: one standard deviation more form gap is worth +0.026 expected points per match (95% interval −0.041 to +0.089).

The market test

The closing market is a tougher control than our model: it has team news and the money of people paid to be right. With the market's probabilities in place of ours the estimate is −0.011 points per match (−0.078 to +0.053). The interval includes zero.

The verdict

No evidence

No evidence. A team in better form by one standard deviation (0.98 points per game over the last five) gains +0.03 expected points per match beyond its strength. Over a season that is between -1.5 and +3.4 points.

Unadjusted p = 0.429; after the Holm correction for testing nine things at once, 1.000. Rule fixed in advance in the pre-registration.

What it means

A run of results mostly tells you how good a team is, and the strength model already knows that. What is left over is too small to find in four seasons.

Verdict rule: "Real effect" needs a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05 and a practically meaningful size; "Small effect" is detectable but small; "No evidence" means the adjusted p is 0.05 or more. Methodology.
