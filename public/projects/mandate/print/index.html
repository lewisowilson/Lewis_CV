Mandate

Insuring the agents that spend your money.

By Lewis Wilson. October 2026.
Mandate is a theoretical model for research and education. It is not an insurance product, quote or financial advice. Landscape statements are as of October 2026. Every parameter is an illustrative assumption. Legal summaries are not legal advice.

Executive summary

Software can now spend money on our behalf. AI agents compare products, fill baskets, book trips and pay supplier invoices, and the payment networks have spent the last eighteen months building the rails to let them check out [M1, M2]. What has not been built is an answer to a simple question: when an agent gets it wrong, who pays?

Today the answer is unclear. The card networks have launched agent frameworks, but liability beyond straightforward fraud remains largely unresolved [M4]. Under UK payment law, a payment is authorised if the payer consented in the agreed way, so a mistake made by an agent you set up may still count as your authorised payment [M6]. Specialist AI insurance exists, but it is written for businesses deploying AI, not for the people relying on it [M10].

This paper designs a theoretical product to fill that gap. Mandate insures people and small businesses against losses caused by AI agents making payments: wrong items, wrong prices, duplicated orders, manipulated agents and counterfeit merchants. It sets out what the cover would include, how claims would interact with existing protections, and, in full, how it would be priced.

The pricing model is deliberately transparent. Premiums are built from the frequency and severity of six types of loss, adjusted by factors the policyholder controls: how much autonomy the agent has, how payments are authorised, which payment rail is used, and what safeguards are switched on. That transparency is the point. If safer configurations are cheaper to insure, insurance becomes a market signal that rewards safer AI.

The model also confronts the feature that makes AI risk unusual: errors are systematic. A single faulty model update can affect thousands of users at once, so an insurer must price correlated losses, not just individual ones. The paper shows how a simple common shock changes the capital an insurer would need.

1Section 1: Introduction: when software holds the card

Delegation is not new. People have always asked others to buy things for them, and the law has long understood agency: if you authorise someone to act for you, their actions bind you within the limits of that authority. What is new is the agent. An AI system can make thousands of decisions a day, at a scale and speed no human assistant could match, and it makes mistakes differently from a person: confidently, repeatedly, and sometimes because someone has deliberately fed it misleading instructions.

The infrastructure for agent payments is arriving quickly. Mastercard launched Agent Pay in April 2025, and Santander and Mastercard completed what they described as Europe's first live end-to-end payment executed by an AI agent, on live payments infrastructure in a controlled pilot, in March 2026 [M1, M2]. Protocols now exist to record what a user actually intended: Google's AP2 uses cryptographic "mandates", signed records of the user's instruction, while OpenAI and Stripe's protocol issues payment tokens scoped to one merchant, capped at a maximum amount and time-limited [M3].

Consumers are not yet convinced. In an April 2026 survey of online adults in the US, UK and Canada, three-quarters said they were uncomfortable with an agent paying autonomously, even with preset spending limits, and they expected the AI platform to be responsible when something went wrong [M21]. Trust, rather than technology, has been identified as the main barrier to agentic commerce [M3].

Insurance has historically been one of the ways societies make new risks acceptable. Motor insurance made cars usable by ordinary people; cyber insurance has shaped how companies secure their systems. This paper asks whether insurance could do the same for agentic payments, and what such a product would need to look like.

2Section 2: How agents pay

An agent payment passes through several layers, each with its own failure points (adapted from [M3]):

  1. Discovery: the agent searches, compares and selects a product or service
  2. Checkout: the agent builds a basket and passes it to the merchant
  3. Authorisation: the agent proves it has permission to pay. This may be a signed mandate, a scoped token, or simply a stored card
  4. Settlement: money moves over a card network, bank transfer or alternative rail

The strength of the authorisation layer matters enormously for insurance. A signed mandate records exactly what the user asked for, so a dispute can compare the instruction with the outcome. A browser agent using a saved card leaves far weaker evidence. The payment rail matters too, because each rail carries different statutory and scheme protections (Section 4).

3Section 3: How agents fail

Agent errors fall into recognisable patterns.

Misinterpretation. The agent buys the wrong size, date, model or quantity. In one shopping-agent benchmark, the most common failure (39.1% of errors) was asserting product attributes that had never been verified [M15].

Price error. Agents can be confidently wrong about prices. One study of AI shopping assistants reportedly found that when prices were wrong, they were wrong by a median of $300 [M18].

Duplication. Retries and timeouts can produce the same order twice.

Manipulation. Agents read web pages, product descriptions and documents, and those can contain instructions aimed at them. In one test, a product description told agents to add an extra item to every order; agents added the item in 40% of sessions, and told the customer it was required [M16].

Counterfeit merchants. Researchers have shown an agentic browser checking out on a counterfeit store using saved card details [M17].

Scope creep. An agent instructed to buy one thing may also sign up to a subscription or add services.

Business payments. For businesses, the stakes are larger. Payments fraud is already widespread: 76% of US organisations experienced attempted or actual payments fraud in 2025, and vendor impersonation is a rising business email compromise tactic [M19]. Security researchers warn that attackers can adapt these techniques to target agents that hold delegated authority to approve payments [M20].

One feature of AI failure matters especially for insurance: it is systematic. A model that makes an error will tend to repeat it until it is corrected [M13]. Human mistakes are largely independent; agent mistakes can be correlated across thousands of users.

4Section 4: Who pays today

UK law allocates payment losses through a patchwork of rules that were designed for human payers.

Consent and authorisation. Under the Payment Services Regulations 2017, a payment is authorised if the payer consented in the form agreed with their payment provider [M6]. If you set up an agent and gave it your payment credentials, its payments may well be authorised, even when the outcome is not what you intended. A commentary on the Oxford Business Law Blog describes the result as a potential liability gap between consumer protection, payment law and product responsibility [M6].

Payment rails. Protection depends heavily on how the payment was made [M6]. Credit card purchases benefit from chargeback rights and, for items priced over £100 and up to £30,000, the card issuer's joint liability under section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act [M22]. Debit cards offer chargebacks but not section 75. Bank transfers offer little protection for errors. Stablecoin rails typically lack chargeback infrastructure altogether [M7].

Fraud reimbursement. Since October 2024, victims of authorised push payment fraud over Faster Payments and CHAPS are entitled to reimbursement of up to £85,000 per claim [M8]. That helps where an agent is tricked into paying a fraudster, but it does not cover an agent that simply buys the wrong thing.

Businesses deploying agents. The CMA's March 2026 guidance makes clear that consumer law applies in the same way whether a customer deals with a human or an AI agent, and that businesses are responsible for what their agents do [M9]. That protects consumers dealing with a retailer's agent. It does less for consumers whose own agent makes the mistake.

The networks. Visa, Mastercard and American Express have launched agent frameworks, but liability between merchant, issuer and agent platform remains largely undecided beyond straightforward fraud [M4]. American Express is reported to have the clearest commitment so far [M5].

The result is a matrix of partial protection. A consumer whose agent makes a mistake on a credit card has a reasonable chance of recovery; the same mistake on a bank transfer or a stablecoin wallet may leave them with nothing.

5Section 5: Why insurance, and why now

Insurance markets for AI are forming, but from the supply side. Munich Re has insured the performance of AI models since 2018, pricing cover on the robustness of the model [M12]. Armilla, which describes itself as the first Lloyd's coverholder dedicated exclusively to AI liability (2024), now covers AI agent failures with limits up to $25 million per organisation [M10]. Traditional policies remain thin on AI: one broker noted that a typical $5 million technology policy might allocate only $25,000 to AI-related liabilities [M11].

These products serve companies that build or deploy AI. No mainstream product protects the individual or small business on the receiving end of their own agent's mistake. That is the gap Mandate is designed for.

Insurance could do more than reimburse losses. Risk-based pricing communicates risk. If a policyholder can see that requiring approval above £50, or using a payment rail with chargeback rights, reduces their premium, the premium becomes a running guide to safer behaviour. If insurers pay claims and then recover from merchants, platforms and networks, they create pressure further up the chain for safer agents. This is the role insurance has played in other emerging risks, and it is the central argument of this paper.

6Section 6: Designing the cover

Mandate covers six insured events for individuals (misinterpretation, price error, duplication, manipulation, counterfeit merchants and scope creep) and adds invoice manipulation, payee substitution and overpayment for businesses (Section 4 of the specification).

4.1

What is covered: people

In plain English
  • Misinterpretation: the wrong item, size, date or quantity.
  • Price error: paying well above your limit or the quoted price.
  • Duplication: the same order placed twice.
  • Manipulation: the agent is prompt-injected into adding items or changing details.
  • Counterfeit merchant: the agent pays a fake store.
  • Scope creep: a subscription or extra service you did not ask for.
  • Booking failure (optional add-on): non-refundable travel booked wrongly, with limited consequential cover.
Illustrative policy wordingThe Insurer will indemnify the Insured against Financial Loss arising from a Payment executed by an Authorised Agent where the Payment departs from the Insured's Instruction by reason of a Covered Peril, as set out in the Schedule.
4.2

What is covered: businesses

In plain EnglishEverything in 4.1, plus an agent paying a tampered or fake invoice, paying a supplier whose bank details were changed by a fraudster, and overpaying an invoice.
Illustrative policy wordingFor a Business Policy, Covered Perils further include Invoice Manipulation, Payee Substitution and Overpayment, each as defined.
4.3

What you pay first, and how claims are settled

In plain English
  • Excess: you pay the first part of each claim.
  • Co-payment: you keep a share of each loss above the excess.
  • Limits: there is a maximum per claim and per year.
  • Recovery first: Mandate pays you promptly, then pursues the merchant, platform, card scheme or bank itself.
  • Evidence: a signed mandate or agent log gets a claim paid faster, with no shared-fault reduction.
  • Shared fault: the payout is reduced if you dismissed the agent's confirmation request or switched off required controls.
Illustrative policy wordingEach Claim is subject to the Excess, the Co-payment and the Limits stated in the Schedule. On payment the Insurer is subrogated to the Insured's rights of recovery. Where the Insured dismissed a confirmation request or disabled a Required Control, the Insurer may reduce the amount payable in proportion to the Insured's contribution to the Loss.
4.4

What is not covered

In plain English
  • Changing your mind when the agent did what you asked.
  • Purchases outside the set-up you declared, for example with caps switched off.
  • Losses already paid back another way.
  • Deliberate misuse.
  • Crypto rails, unless you add them.
Illustrative policy wordingThe Insurer shall not be liable for (a) any Payment that conforms to the Instruction; (b) any Payment made outside the Declared Configuration; (c) any Loss already reimbursed from another source; (d) any deliberate act of the Insured; or (e) any Payment on a crypto-asset rail, unless the Crypto Rail Extension is shown in the Schedule.
4.5

What Mandate requires

In plain English
  • A spending cap on every transaction.
  • Agent logs kept for 90 days.
  • Telling Mandate promptly about a suspected loss, for example within 30 days.
Illustrative policy wordingIt is a condition precedent to liability that the Insured maintain a per-transaction Spending Cap, retain Agent Logs for not less than ninety days, and notify the Insurer of any suspected Loss within thirty days of discovery.

Three design choices do most of the work.

Pay first, recover later. Policyholders should not have to navigate chargebacks, section 75 claims and platform complaints before being paid. Mandate pays promptly and then pursues recoveries, so its net cost depends on the payment rail.

Evidence reduces friction. Claims supported by a signed mandate or agent log can be assessed by comparing instruction and outcome. These claims are paid faster and without any shared-fault reduction. This creates a reason to choose agents that keep good records.

Shared responsibility, not blame. An excess, a modest co-payment and a shared-fault reduction (for example, where the policyholder dismissed a confirmation request) keep the policyholder engaged without undermining the cover.

Exclusions are designed to keep the product insurable: buyer's remorse where the agent followed instructions correctly, purchases outside the agreed configuration, and deliberate misuse.

Peril rows show expected events a year and expected net cost a year. Figures are rounded; totals use unrounded values.

Frequency. For each type of loss, the expected number of events in a year is the number of agent transactions multiplied by a base probability that a transaction produces an unrecovered problem, adjusted by rating factors. More autonomy, weaker authorisation, an open web of merchants and weak defences against manipulation all increase frequency. Approvals, signed mandates, merchant allowlists and good defences reduce it.

Severity. The size of each loss is modelled with a lognormal distribution, a skewed shape that captures many small losses and a few large ones, scaled to the policyholder's typical purchase and capped by their spending limit. Spending caps therefore act directly on severity: the single most effective safeguard in the model.

Recoveries. Expected recoveries depend on the payment rail. Credit cards recover a large share of losses; stablecoins recover none. Because Mandate pays first, recoveries reduce its net cost rather than the policyholder's payout.

Payout. Each loss passes through the excess, co-payment, per-claim limit and any shared-fault reduction, and annual payouts are capped.

Premium. The pure premium is the expected net cost. To it are added a risk margin proportional to the variability of losses, and expenses. Claim-free years reduce the premium through a bonus-malus scale.

Every parameter in the model is illustrative. No insurer has yet published loss data for agentic payments, and the paper does not pretend otherwise. The value of the model lies in its structure: it shows which factors drive the risk, how they interact, and how much each safeguard is worth.

8Section 8: The systemic problem

Most personal insurance relies on independence: one person's burst pipe says little about another's. AI agent losses break this assumption. AI errors recur until they are corrected [M13]. Agents built on the same model share the same weaknesses, so a single faulty update or a widely circulated manipulation technique could trigger losses across thousands of policies at once.

The model captures this with a common shock: a small annual probability of a systemic event that multiplies error rates across the whole portfolio for a short period. Even a modest shock dramatically widens the tail of the insurer's loss distribution and increases the capital needed to remain solvent.

Insurer view

The web version simulates a 10,000-policy portfolio with and without the common shock and reports the capital an insurer would need (99.5% value at risk). See Methodology for the model.

This has practical consequences. A real Mandate would need aggregation limits, clear event definitions (for example, treating losses from one model version as one event), reinsurance, and pricing that reflects concentration across agent providers. It would also have a strong interest in monitoring agent providers and in recovering systemic losses from them. Cyber insurance offers the closest precedent, and its early history suggests that insurers who ignore correlation learn about it expensively.

9Section 9: Businesses

For businesses, the dominant risk is not a wrong pair of trainers but a wrong payee. Agents that read invoices, match them to orders and pay suppliers create a single point of failure that attackers are expected to target [M19, M20]. The business version of Mandate therefore prices controls familiar to any finance team: segregation of duties, verification of changed bank details, and e-invoicing, each of which sharply reduces modelled fraud frequency. The lesson matches long-standing finance practice: automation is safest when it inherits good controls, not when it replaces them.

10Section 10: Limitations and open questions

  • Data. Parameters are illustrative. Real pricing would require loss data that does not yet exist publicly.
  • Law. The legal map is a summary and is moving quickly; network liability rules in particular are still being written [M4].
  • Moral hazard and adverse selection. Pricing and evidence requirements reduce, but do not eliminate, these classic problems.
  • Regulation. A real product would require authorisation and would sit under the FCA's Consumer Duty.
  • Who should insure. It is possible that the natural home for this cover is the payment network or agent platform rather than a standalone insurer, as consumer expectations suggest [M21]. Mandate's pricing logic would apply either way.

11Section 11: Conclusion

Agentic payments are arriving faster than the rules that govern them. Until liability is settled, the people who use agents carry a risk they can neither see nor price. Mandate shows that this risk can be described, measured and priced with familiar tools, and that doing so openly would reward the safest ways of letting software spend our money. Whether or not a product like Mandate is ever sold, the exercise points to a practical principle for the industry: build agents whose instructions are recorded, whose spending is bounded, and whose mistakes can be traced. Those are the agents that will be insurable, and the ones people will trust.
