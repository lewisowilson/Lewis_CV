The science behind each step

Four ideas carry the concept: reading blood flow with a laser, where capillaries sit in skin, how small they are next to a needle, and a valve made of wax.

Reading blood flow with laser speckle

Shine a laser on skin and the light scattered back interferes with itself, making a grainy pattern called speckle. Where red blood cells move, the pattern flickers, and during one camera exposure the flicker blurs it. Laser speckle contrast imaging measures that blur as a contrast, K = σ / ⟨I⟩, the standard deviation of brightness over a small window divided by its mean[7]. Low contrast means fast flow.

The full model links contrast to how fast the speckle decorrelates and to the exposure time[7]. For exposures much longer than that decorrelation time, 1/K² is close to proportional to flow, which is the perfusion index the simulator reports. Try a very short exposure in the figure: the vessel and tissue start to look alike, because neither has time to blur.

The problem with holding it

A handheld imager moves. In the University of Twente's handheld perfusion imager study, on-surface speeds were 8.5 times higher handheld than on a stand, and after correction the handheld and mounted readings differed by 16.4 ± 9.3%[7]. Aligning frames before averaging them sharpened the maps, and after processing the two agreed on a visual basis[7].

It has been done outside the lab

The same group has since used a wireless handheld imager in 15 patients during breast reconstruction surgery, and stressed that motion correction is needed to measure reliably[8]. A low-cost speckle imager built around a mobile phone was demonstrated in 2014[9]. None of this has been done inside a patch workflow; that is part of what the concept would need to prove.

Skin layers and capillaries

Below the outer layers of skin, capillary loops rise into small ridges of the dermis, the papillae, from a plexus of larger vessels further down[13]. One skin model places the loops about 150 µm below the junction between epidermis and dermis, with a thin layer free of vessels above them[13]. Density varies: about 7 capillaries per mm² at rest by in vivo confocal microscopy, rising when skin is warmed[10], and over 40 per mm² in psoriatic plaques[11]. It also falls with age[12].

Capillaries against needle scale

Microneedles are typically 50 to 900 µm long and under 300 µm wide, long enough to cross the outer barrier of the skin without reaching the nerve endings deeper in the dermis[14]. A capillary loop is about 10 µm across[10].

So the patch does not aim needles into capillaries. The claim is coarser and easier to defend: choosing which channels to open puts drug beside the richest networks under the patch, rather than evenly across rich and poor skin.

A valve made of wax

Paraffin valves are a known part of lab-on-chip devices. In a close-open valve, a block of paraffin seals a channel; a heater melts it, pressure pushes it downstream, and it sets again on the wall of a wider section, leaving the channel open[19]. Phase-change valves like these are described as relatively new and cheap[20].

The concept puts one plug in every channel of the patch and the heaters in the reusable scanner, so the disposable part needs no valve electronics[25]. Valves of this kind have not been made at patch scale; that is an open engineering question, covered on the hard parts page.
