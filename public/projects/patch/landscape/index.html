The landscape

Patches that deliver through the skin are already in trials. All of them deliver a fixed dose. None reads the skin before deciding.

Microneedle vaccines

Micron Biomedical

A microneedle patch for measles and rubella, tested in a phase 1/2 trial in The Gambia. In infants, 93% seroconverted to measles and 100% to rubella after the patch, against 90% and 100% after injection, and all local reactions were mild[6].

Vaxxas

A high-density microarray patch. In a phase 1 influenza trial, 2.5 µg of antigen by patch gave antibody levels not significantly different from 15 µg injected into muscle, and the coated vaccine was stable at 40°C for at least 12 months. Local reactions were mostly redness at the site[21].

Vaxess, now Terrestrial

vaxess.com now redirects to Terrestrial, which describes a microarray patch with dissolvable tips that needs no refrigeration and is designed to be self-administered at home[22]. The company's trial press releases could not be opened, so no results are quoted here.

What these trials show: for these vaccines, a patch produced immune responses comparable to injection, with mild local effects. What they do not do: adapt the dose or watch for a reaction. A vaccine is usually given once or a few times; immunotherapy is given many times over years.

Patches for allergy

Moonlight Therapeutics. Children's Healthcare of Atlanta opened a first-in-human phase 1 trial of Moonlight's microneedle skin stamp for peanut immunotherapy in 2026. Doses start at 1 µg under supervision and rise weekly to 100 µg if tolerated. The investigator expects the next study to be supervised too; home use is a goal for later[5].

DBV Technologies, VIASKIN Peanut. An epicutaneous patch: allergen delivered through the skin with no needles at all. DBV submitted a licence application to the US FDA in September 2026 for children aged 4 to 7, supported by a phase 3 trial in 654 children[23].

The gap the concept aims at is the one the Moonlight investigator names: getting from supervised dosing to home use. The concept's answer is to add sensing and a gate to the patch itself. Whether that would ever be enough to allow home dosing is a question for clinical studies and regulators, not for this site.
