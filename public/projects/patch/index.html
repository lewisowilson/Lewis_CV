A patch that maps your skin, tests itself, then doses.

A research concept for allergy immunotherapy at home: scan the skin, open the right valves, test a micro-dose, then decide.

06 Dose or abort

An hour in the clinic after every injection, for three years

Allergen immunotherapy is the only treatment that can modify the allergic response itself[1]. Given as injections under the skin (subcutaneous immunotherapy, SCIT), it means regular supervised visits, because each injection carries a small risk of a severe reaction[3].

60 min

observation after every SCIT injection under the BSACI procedure[1]. An NHS leaflet puts the whole appointment at about 90 minutes[3].

3 years

the usual length of a course in the UK[2].

23 to 55%

published adherence to SCIT[4].

I researched why people stop allergy immunotherapy and found that many give up because of the time commitment of repeated, supervised clinic visits.

Lewis Wilson

At a large German allergy centre, 34.8% of patients stopped before finishing the three-year course (32.4% of those on SCIT), most of them in the first year[4]. Among those who gave a reason, moving or travel and long waiting hours together made up 40%. Germany asks patients to wait at least 30 minutes after each injection[4]; the UK asks for 60.

The question this concept asks: could the first, riskiest minutes after a dose be watched by a device instead, so that more of the course could happen at home? It is a design question, not a claim that it would be safe.

How it would work

Two parts: a reusable handheld scanner and a disposable microneedle patch with two reservoirs and a wax valve on every channel[25]. Scroll through the six steps; each one has a control that changes the cross-section.

01 Scan

The handheld scanner shines a laser onto the skin under the patch footprint and films the speckle pattern it makes. Moving red blood cells blur the speckle during each exposure, so more blur means more flow. Turning blur into a number gives a map of perfusion: blood flow across the patch area[7].

This is laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI). A handheld LSCI imager has already been compared with a mounted one on skin[7].

The figure marks the capillary-rich zones in accent.

02 Select

The software picks the valves that sit over the most capillary-rich zones. The drug is deposited next to dense capillary networks, not inside capillaries: a capillary loop is about 10 µm across[10], while microneedles are typically 50 to 900 µm long and up to 300 µm wide[14].

03 Open

Each channel is blocked by a paraffin plug. Before the patch goes on, the scanner heats only the chosen valve positions for 2 to 3 seconds; the wax melts at about 42°C and the channel stays open[25]. There are no electronics inside the valve. Paraffin valves that melt open like this are established in lab-on-chip devices[19, 20].

04 Test

The patch is applied. A small test reservoir releases a micro-dose through a subset of the open channels. The full-dose reservoir stays closed.

05 Read

The scanner watches the skin for about 15 minutes, the same window used to read a skin prick test[15]. It looks for a local reaction: a flare of increased blood flow, a raised wheal, or both. It reads a zone around the test site rather than a single point, because reactions spread.

Skin response

06 Dose or abort

If the zone stays clear, the full-dose reservoir releases through the open channels. If a reaction crosses the thresholds, delivery halts and the user is alerted.

A 15-minute read cannot see reactions that start later. UK guidance observes patients for 60 minutes after every injection[1], so the gate is a way to reduce risk that would need clinical validation, not a replacement for safety rules.

What the read found

What is new, and what is not

Valves on microneedle devices are not new: an existing patent notes that valves in such a device may be activated thermally[18]. The claim here is the combination: perfusion-mapped choice of channels, a test dose that gates the full dose, and a read-out by the same sensor, in a patch a person applies themselves. Microneedle patches in trials today deliver one dose with no feedback[6, 21, 5].
